# Managers pathway

> **Who this is for:** Team leaders, line managers, service and operations managers, and project leads responsible for how a team's work gets done.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

Managers are where AI adoption succeeds or stalls. Leaders can buy tools and set policy, but whether those tools improve how work is done depends on the people who organise that work day to day. This pathway covers helping your team adopt AI tools safely, redesigning workflows rather than bolting AI onto old ones, leading people through change, and thinking clearly about performance and quality. It applies whether you manage three people or three hundred.

## What AI adoption means for managers

- **Adoption is uneven by default.** Some team members will experiment enthusiastically; others will avoid the tools or use them in ways that create risk. Employer encouragement makes a large difference: one large study found adoption rose from around half of workers to over 80% when employers encouraged use [1].
- **Benefits vary by person and task.** Several studies found that less experienced workers gained most on tasks the tools handle well [2][3], but other research found that the same tool helped strong performers and harmed weaker ones when the task was open-ended and users could not judge the advice [4]. You need to know which situation your team is in.
- **Tools change individual work more easily than shared work.** A large field experiment with an AI assistant built into office software found people spent less time on email but meeting time did not change; the authors concluded the tool mainly affected work individuals could change on their own [5]. Redesigning shared processes is a management task.
- **Quality responsibility stays with you.** AI output can be fluent and wrong. On a task just beyond the tool's capabilities, consultants using AI were less likely to reach the correct answer than those without it [3].

## Your key responsibilities and decisions

| Responsibility | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **Identify where AI helps your team** | Map tasks, find candidates, test them on real examples |
| **Redesign workflows** | Decide where AI fits, where people review, and what changes upstream and downstream |
| **Set team norms** | Translate organisational policy into practical "how we use AI here" guidance |
| **Support skills and confidence** | Make time for learning, pair experienced and new users, share what works |
| **Protect quality** | Define review steps and quality checks for AI-assisted work |
| **Lead change** | Communicate honestly, listen to concerns, involve the team in decisions |
| **Manage performance fairly** | Adjust expectations based on evidence, not assumptions |
| **Escalate risks** | Report incidents, near misses and policy gaps |

### Workflow redesign: a simple method

1. **Map the current process** as a sequence of tasks, noting who does each, how long it takes, and where errors occur.
2. **Classify each task** using the fit test in [capabilities and limitations](../foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md#is-this-task-a-good-fit-a-practical-test): is the output easy to check? Is the cost of an error low? Does the person have the expertise to spot mistakes?
3. **Choose a pattern for each candidate task:**

| Pattern | Description | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| **AI drafts, person finalises** | AI produces a first version; a person edits and approves | Writing, summaries, code, reports |
| **Person drafts, AI reviews** | A person produces the work; AI checks for gaps, errors or clarity | High-stakes documents where human authorship matters |
| **AI triages, person decides** | AI sorts, tags or prioritises; a person makes the decision | Inboxes, tickets, document review |
| **AI as tutor or explainer** | AI helps a person understand something | Onboarding, unfamiliar topics |
| **No AI** | Task stays as it is | Tasks failing the fit test, or decisions about individuals without specific approval |

4. **Look at the whole flow.** If drafting gets faster, does review become the bottleneck? Do downstream teams need to change?
5. **Pilot with the team**, measure against a baseline, and adjust. See [pilot](../lifecycle/04-pilot.md).

## Questions you should be asking

**About your team's work**
- Which tasks take the most time, and which of those pass the fit test?
- Where would an undetected AI error cause real harm?
- What is already being done with AI, informally?

**About your people**
- Who is confident with these tools, and who is anxious or sceptical? Why?
- Do junior staff still get the practice they need to build expertise?
- What do people fear: job loss, monitoring, being judged for using or not using AI?

**About quality and performance**
- How will we check AI-assisted work, and who is accountable for it?
- What evidence would justify changing workload expectations or targets?
- How will time saved be used?

## Your reading route

1. **Understand the tools (30 minutes).** Read [capabilities and limitations](../foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md), focusing on the jagged frontier and the fit test.
2. **Know the rules.** Read your organisation's AI policy and acceptable use policy, or the [acceptable use policy template](../../toolkits/acceptable-use-policy.md).
3. **Find and prioritise use cases.** Use [Discover](../lifecycle/01-discover.md) and the [use case prioritisation](../../assessments/use-case-prioritisation.md) assessment with your team.
4. **Check the data.** If use cases depend on team documents or systems, run the [data readiness](../../assessments/data-readiness.md) check with your data or IT colleagues.
5. **Plan and run a pilot.** Read [Pilot](../lifecycle/04-pilot.md) and use the [pilot plan template](../../toolkits/pilot-plan-template.md).
6. **Measure properly.** Read [measuring value](../measurement/measuring-value.md), especially baselines and vanity metrics.
7. **Lead the change.** Use the [communication plan template](../../toolkits/communication-plan-template.md) and [training plan template](../../toolkits/training-plan-template.md). Read the [people and learning pathway](people-and-learning.md) for consultation and job design.
8. **Capture and share what works.** Start a team [prompt library](../../toolkits/prompt-library-template.md).
9. **Scale and sustain.** Read [Scale](../lifecycle/05-scale.md) and [Operate](../lifecycle/06-operate.md).

## Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Expecting immediate productivity gains and raising targets | Measure first; adjust expectations on evidence; allow for a learning period |
| Rolling out tools without changing the process | Redesign the workflow; decide where review happens |
| Leaving adoption to enthusiasts | Make time for everyone to learn; pair people; share examples |
| Treating AI use as cheating, or as compulsory | Set clear norms on where AI is expected, allowed and not allowed |
| Relying on staff saying it helps | Combine satisfaction with measured time and quality |
| Junior staff skipping the learning | Design tasks so that people still build core skills; use AI as a tutor, not just a shortcut |
| Review becoming a rubber stamp | Use checklists; sample-check reviewed work; rotate reviewers |
| Ignoring anxiety | Talk openly about what will and will not change; involve the team in design |

## What good looks like

- The team has a short, shared "how we use AI" note aligned with organisational policy.
- Each AI-assisted workflow has a named owner, a defined review step, and a measure of quality.
- People have dedicated time to learn, and know who to ask for help.
- The manager can say, with evidence, which tasks AI has improved, which it has not, and what happened to the time saved.
- Concerns are raised openly and acted on.
- Incidents and near misses are reported without blame and lead to improvements.
- In small teams: a weekly ten-minute "what worked, what didn't" conversation can be enough.
