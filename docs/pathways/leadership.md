# Leadership pathway

> **Who this is for:** Chief executives, directors, board members, trustees, partners and owners of small businesses: anyone who sets direction, approves investment and is accountable for outcomes.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

For leaders, AI adoption is a strategic and governance question before it is a technology one. Your job is to decide why your organisation is adopting AI tools, what it is willing to risk, how much to invest and on what evidence, and who is accountable for what. This pathway sets out those decisions, the questions to ask your teams and suppliers, and a reading route through the guide. It is relevant to a ten-person business and a multinational alike; the scale of the answers differs, but the questions do not.

## What AI adoption means for leaders

- **A strategic choice, not a default.** AI tools can improve speed, quality and capacity on some tasks, but rigorous research shows effects are uneven, and gains on individual tasks are much smaller at the level of whole organisations unless work is redesigned [1][2]. See [evidence on impact](../foundations/evidence-on-impact.md).
- **Accountability that cannot be delegated to a tool.** Your organisation remains responsible for decisions, advice and content produced with AI assistance, including to regulators, customers and courts.
- **Use is already happening.** Survey research in Denmark found that around half of workers in AI-exposed occupations had used AI chatbots for work even without employer encouragement, rising to over 80% where employers encouraged it [2]. Choosing not to decide still leaves you with unmanaged use.
- **Legal duties may already apply.** For example, if your organisation uses AI systems within the scope of the EU AI Act, it has had obligations to take measures on staff AI literacy since 2 February 2025 [3]. See [regulation overview](../governance/regulation-overview.md).

### Setting risk appetite in practice

A risk appetite statement for AI does not need to be long. A useful one separates uses into tiers. The example below is illustrative; adapt it to your sector and jurisdiction.

| Tier | Example uses | Appetite | Typical controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Drafting internal documents, summarising non-confidential material, brainstorming | Open: encourage use in approved tools | Acceptable use policy, training |
| Medium | Customer-facing drafts reviewed by staff, analysis of confidential business data, code generation | Cautious: approve per use case | Risk assessment, human review, logging |
| High | Decisions about individuals (hiring, credit, eligibility), safety-critical or legally binding outputs | Averse: only with specific approval, if at all | Full risk assessment, DPIA, legal review, ongoing monitoring |
| Prohibited | Uses banned by law or by your principles | None | Policy prohibition |

## Questions you should be asking

**Of your leadership team**

- Which two or three business outcomes should AI help with, and how will we know if it has?
- What are we not prepared to use AI for, and why?
- Who is accountable for AI governance, and do they have the authority and time?
- What are staff already using, and what data is going into it?

**Of proposals and business cases**

- Is the expected benefit based on evidence from comparable tasks, or on vendor claims or headline figures?
- What is the baseline, and how will we estimate what would have happened anyway?
- What are the total costs, including integration, training, governance and staff time, not just licences?
- How will time saved be used? Is the benefit cash-releasing or capacity?
- What would make us stop?

**Of suppliers**

- How is our data used, stored and retained, and is it used to train models?
- What usage, audit and outcome data can you give us?
- What happens to price and terms at renewal, and how easily can we leave?

**Of risk, legal and compliance**

- Which regulations apply to our planned uses, and in which jurisdictions?
- Which uses would need a DPIA or other formal assessment?

## Your reading route

1. **Get the grounding (45 minutes).** Read [capabilities and limitations](../foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md) and [evidence on impact](../foundations/evidence-on-impact.md). These will equip you to challenge over-optimistic claims and over-cautious ones.
2. **Locate your organisation.** Ask a small group to complete the [organisational readiness](../../assessments/organisational-readiness.md) assessment and place you on the [maturity model](../../assessments/maturity-model.md).
3. **Set direction.** Read [Discover](../lifecycle/01-discover.md) and use [use case prioritisation](../../assessments/use-case-prioritisation.md) to choose where to start.
4. **Set up governance proportionate to your size.** Read [governance model](../governance/governance-model.md) and [responsible AI principles](../governance/responsible-ai-principles.md). Adopt or adapt the [AI policy template](../../toolkits/ai-policy-template.md) and agree roles with the [RACI template](../../toolkits/raci-template.md).
5. **Understand your obligations.** Skim the [regulation overview](../governance/regulation-overview.md) and [risk management](../governance/risk-management.md), and ask your risk lead which apply.
6. **Approve investment on evidence.** Use the [business case template](../../toolkits/business-case-template.md) and insist on a [pilot](../lifecycle/04-pilot.md) with a measurement plan from [measuring value](../measurement/measuring-value.md).
7. **Plan for scale and operation.** Read [Scale](../lifecycle/05-scale.md) and [Operate](../lifecycle/06-operate.md), focusing on decision points and ongoing oversight.
8. **Make the people commitments.** Read the [people and learning pathway](people-and-learning.md) summary and agree your approach to consultation and changing roles.

## Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Buying licences before defining purpose | Fear of falling behind; vendor pressure | Start from outcomes and use cases; pilot first |
| Targets based on headline figures | Studies of single tasks are quoted as organisation-wide gains | Use ranges from comparable tasks and measure your own results |
| Treating AI as an IT project | It looks like a software rollout | Give a business owner accountability; involve HR, risk and managers from the start |
| Banning use outright | Risk aversion | Bans tend to drive use underground. Provide an approved, controlled alternative |
| Measuring success by usage | Easy to report | Measure outcomes: quality, efficiency, risk and experience |
| Ignoring the workforce question | Uncomfortable | Be explicit about intentions; consult early; invest in skills |
| No exit route | Enthusiasm and sunk cost | Agree stop criteria in advance for each use case |

## What good looks like

- A short, published statement of why the organisation is using AI, where it will and will not use it, and who is accountable.
- A risk appetite that distinguishes types of use, known to staff.
- A small portfolio of prioritised use cases, each with an owner, hypothesis, baseline and stop criteria, recorded in a [use case register](../../toolkits/use-case-register-template.md).
- Investment released in stages, against measured results rather than projections.
- Regular board or leadership reporting covering value, risk incidents and staff experience, not just adoption numbers.
- Staff who know what tools they may use, with what data, and where to get help.
- For small organisations: a one-page policy, business-grade tools with controls switched on, and a named person responsible.
