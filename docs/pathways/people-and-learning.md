# People and learning pathway

> **Who this is for:** HR and people teams, learning and development, organisational development, internal communications, employee relations, and anyone responsible for skills, job design or workforce planning.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

AI tools change what people do at work, what skills they need, and how they feel about their jobs. People and learning teams shape whether that change is handled well: building skills and AI literacy, redesigning roles, consulting the workforce, supporting managers through change, and making sure AI used *in* HR processes is fair and lawful. This pathway sets out those responsibilities, the evidence that should inform them, and a reading route through the guide.

## What AI adoption means for people and learning teams

- **Skills matter more, not less.** Several experiments found that the benefits of AI tools depend on the user's expertise and approach: in one, high-performing entrepreneurs benefited while low performers were harmed [1]; in another, participants with less formal education gained most while using AI, but a substantial gap reappeared once the tool was removed, and lasting benefit depended on sustained personal effort [2].
- **Learning can be undermined.** In a field experiment with high-school students, unrestricted use of a chat assistant during practice improved practice scores but lowered later exam performance by 17% compared with students who had no access; a tutor-style design with safeguards largely avoided this [3]. The implications for workplace onboarding and early-career development deserve attention.
- **Confidence affects critical thinking.** A survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more [4].
- **Entry-level roles may be most affected.** Analysis of US payroll data found a 13% relative decline in employment for 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, concentrated where AI automates rather than augments work [5]. This is a single observational study and its causal interpretation is debated, but it is relevant to early-career pipelines and workforce planning.
- **AI literacy may be a legal expectation.** Under Article 4 of the EU AI Act, providers and deployers of AI systems have had an AI literacy obligation since 2 February 2025 [6]. Amendments in force from 27 July 2026 reworded it as a duty to take measures to support staff AI literacy, rather than to ensure a set level for each person; the [regulation overview](../governance/regulation-overview.md#ai-literacy-article-4) has the current position.
- **Employees want to be consulted.** The UK's Acas advises employers to discuss AI plans early with staff and their representatives, consult on its introduction, and develop clear policies, noting that requiring some roles to use AI could amount to a change in terms and conditions [7].

## Your key responsibilities and decisions

### Skills and AI literacy

Define what different groups need to know. An illustrative structure:

| Audience | Core content |
|---|---|
| **Everyone** | What AI tools can and cannot do; the organisation's policy; data handling; verification; how to report concerns |
| **Regular users** | Effective prompting; using tools within specific workflows; recognising errors and bias |
| **Managers** | Workflow redesign; leading change; quality and performance management with AI |
| **Specialists** (IT, data, developers, risk) | Role-specific technical and governance content (see their pathways) |
| **Leaders** | Strategy, risk appetite, accountability, evidence |

Design principles (practitioner consensus, consistent with the evidence above):

- **Practice on real tasks** rather than generic demonstrations.
- **Teach verification as a core skill**, not an afterthought.
- **Protect skill development.** For new starters and early-career staff, design tasks so AI supports learning (explaining, giving feedback) rather than replacing the practice they need.
- **Use peer learning**: champions, communities of practice and shared prompt libraries.
- **Refresh regularly**, because tools change.

Use the [training plan template](../../toolkits/training-plan-template.md).

### Job and work design

- Work with managers to map how tasks within roles change (see the workflow redesign method in the [managers pathway](managers.md)).
- Update role profiles, competency frameworks and career paths where tasks shift substantially.
- Consider what happens to entry-level roles that traditionally built expertise through tasks AI now does. How will people gain that expertise?
- Decide how time released will be used: higher-value work, development, capacity for growth, or changes in resourcing. Be honest about which.

### Workforce consultation and employee relations

| Step | Practice |
|---|---|
| Early engagement | Discuss intentions with staff and representatives before decisions are fixed |
| Formal consultation | Where changes affect terms and conditions, roles or monitoring, follow applicable legal requirements and collective agreements |
| Transparency | Explain what tools are being introduced, why, what data they use, and what will not change |
| Monitoring | Be clear whether and how AI usage data will be used in relation to individuals; involve data protection colleagues |
| Voice | Provide channels for concerns and ideas, and show how feedback is used |

Requirements vary by jurisdiction (for example, works council rights in some EU countries), so take local employment law advice.

### AI in HR processes

Uses of AI in recruitment, performance management, promotion, allocation of work, monitoring and termination carry high legal and ethical risk. Under the EU AI Act, many employment-related uses are classed as high-risk [8]. Research has also documented covert bias in language models' judgements about people [9]. For these uses:

- Complete an [AI risk assessment](../../assessments/ai-risk-assessment.md) and a DPIA.
- Test for bias across groups.
- Keep a human accountable for decisions, with genuine ability to override.
- Tell candidates and employees when AI is used, as required by law.
- Check supplier claims about fairness and accuracy independently.

### Change management

Use the [communication plan template](../../toolkits/communication-plan-template.md). Effective communication about AI tends to:

- Be specific about what is changing, for whom, and when.
- Acknowledge uncertainty honestly.
- Address job security directly rather than avoiding it.
- Share evidence, including limitations.
- Celebrate real examples from colleagues, not only vendor case studies.

## Questions you should be asking

- What level of AI literacy does each group need, and how will we know they have it?
- How are we protecting the development of early-career staff?
- Which roles will change most, and have we involved the people in them?
- Have we met our consultation obligations, and are we going beyond the minimum where it helps trust?
- Is AI being used in any HR decision, including through features in our HR software, and has it been assessed?
- How will AI usage data be used in relation to individuals, and have we told people?
- What have we committed to about how time saved will be used?

## Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| One-off generic training | Role-specific, task-based, refreshed learning |
| Teaching prompts but not verification | Make verification and judgement central |
| Ignoring early-career development | Design for skill building, not just output |
| Avoiding the job security conversation | Address it honestly and early |
| Consulting after decisions are made | Engage before plans are fixed |
| Unassessed AI features in HR software | Inventory and assess HR system features |
| Using usage data to judge individuals without telling them | Be transparent; involve data protection colleagues |

## What good looks like

- A tiered AI literacy programme with completion and competence tracked.
- Early-career development deliberately protected.
- Role changes designed with the people affected, and reflected in role profiles and career paths.
- Consultation that meets legal requirements and builds trust.
- No AI used in HR decisions without risk assessment, DPIA, bias testing and human accountability.
- Regular pulse measures of confidence, workload and concerns, acted upon.
- In small organisations: a short briefing for everyone, a named person to ask, an honest conversation about how roles may change, and a check that no AI is making decisions about people.
