# Everyday users pathway

> **Who this is for:** Anyone who uses, or is about to use, AI tools in their day-to-day work, in any role and any size of organisation.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

AI tools can save you time and help you do better work, but only if you use them for the right tasks, protect the data you handle, and check what they produce. This pathway gives you the essentials: what the tools are good and bad at, how to handle data safely, how to verify output, and how to write prompts that get useful results. It takes about 30 minutes to read, and links to more detail where you want it.

## What AI adoption means for you

- **You are responsible for your work, whether or not AI helped.** If an AI tool gets something wrong and you pass it on, the error is yours.
- **The tools are good at some tasks and unreliable at others.** In one study, consultants using AI did much better work on tasks the AI handled well, but were less likely to get the right answer on a task just outside its abilities [1]. The tricky part is that the two kinds of task can look similar.
- **Feeling faster is not the same as being faster.** In a study of experienced software developers, people believed AI had sped them up when it had actually slowed them down [2]. Notice what actually helps.
- **Your expertise matters.** The benefit you get depends on being able to tell good output from bad [3].
- **There are rules.** Your organisation should have a policy on which tools you may use and what data you can put into them. Follow it, and ask if anything is unclear.

## The five essentials

### 1. Use approved tools

- Use the tools your organisation has approved, through your work account.
- Do not use personal accounts for work data. Consumer versions of tools often have different data terms from business versions.
- If you need a tool that is not approved, ask. Do not work around the rules.

### 2. Protect data

**Before you paste anything in, ask: would I be comfortable if this appeared outside the organisation?**

| Generally do not enter, unless your policy explicitly allows it for that tool | Usually fine in an approved tool |
|---|---|
| Personal data about customers, colleagues or members of the public | Public information |
| Health, financial or other sensitive personal details | Your own drafts with no personal or confidential data |
| Confidential business information (pricing, contracts, strategy, unpublished results) | General questions and explanations |
| Passwords, keys, access codes | Generic templates and examples |
| Legally privileged material | Content already approved for use with that tool |
| Anything a client or partner has asked you to keep confidential | |

If you are unsure, remove names and identifying details, or ask your manager or data protection contact.

### 3. Verify before you rely

AI tools can produce confident, fluent text that is wrong. This is often called "hallucination". It includes invented facts, figures, quotes, references and legal citations. In 2025 the High Court of England and Wales addressed lawyers' use of fictitious case citations in court documents [4].

**A verification checklist:**

- [ ] Have I checked every specific fact, figure, date and name against a reliable source?
- [ ] Do the references and links exist, and do they say what is claimed?
- [ ] Does the output actually answer the question, and has anything important been left out?
- [ ] Is anything biased, unfair or inappropriate for the audience?
- [ ] Would I be happy to put my name to this?
- [ ] Is this an output where an error would cause harm? If so, has someone else checked it?

Check more thoroughly when the stakes are higher. A brainstorm needs little checking; a customer letter, a report to the board or anything involving money, law, health or safety needs careful checking.

### 4. Choose suitable tasks

| Often good uses | Use with care | Avoid unless specifically approved |
|---|---|---|
| First drafts of emails, documents and posts | Summaries of long or complex documents (check for omissions) | Making decisions about individuals (hiring, performance, eligibility) |
| Rewriting for tone, clarity or length | Research questions (check sources) | Legal, medical or financial advice to others |
| Brainstorming ideas and options | Calculations and data analysis (check the numbers) | Anything you cannot check yourself |
| Summarising your own notes | Translation for external use (have it reviewed) | Content that must be original for legal or contractual reasons |
| Explaining a concept or a piece of jargon | Writing code (review and test it) | |
| Turning notes into a structured format | | |

For a fuller test, see [capabilities and limitations](../foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md#is-this-task-a-good-fit-a-practical-test).

### 5. Be open about AI use

- Follow your organisation's rules on disclosing AI use, internally and to customers.
- Do not present AI-generated work as entirely your own where that would mislead.
- Report mistakes, near misses and concerns. They help everyone improve.

## Prompting basics

A prompt is what you type into the tool. Better prompts get better results. A simple structure:

| Element | What to include | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Role or perspective** | Who should it write as or for? | "You are helping a customer service team at a housing association." |
| **Task** | What exactly do you want? | "Draft a reply to the email below." |
| **Context** | Background it needs | "The tenant reported a repair two weeks ago. An engineer is booked for Thursday." |
| **Source material** | Paste or attach what it should use (within data rules) | The email text, with personal details removed |
| **Format** | Length, structure, tone | "Under 150 words, friendly but professional, plain English." |
| **Constraints** | What to avoid | "Do not promise anything not stated above." |

**Tips:**

- **Give it the source.** Asking it to work from material you provide is more reliable than relying on what it "knows".
- **Ask for options.** "Give me three versions" helps you choose.
- **Iterate.** Treat the first answer as a draft. Tell it what to change.
- **Ask it to critique.** "What are the weaknesses of this argument?" Tools tend to agree with you unless asked to challenge.
- **Ask it to show uncertainty.** "Tell me if you are unsure about anything, and which parts I should check."
- **Start a new conversation for a new task**, so earlier material does not confuse it.
- **Save prompts that work** and share them with your team. See the [prompt library template](../../toolkits/prompt-library-template.md).

**Example: weak and strong prompts**

> Weak: "Write a report about our sales."

> Stronger: "Using the figures in the table below, write a one-page summary for the leadership team of sales performance in the third quarter compared with the second. Use three short sections: headline results, main changes, and questions to discuss. Plain English, no jargon. Do not add figures that are not in the table."

## Questions you should be asking

- Which tools am I allowed to use, and with what data?
- Is this a task where I can check the output properly?
- What would happen if this output were wrong?
- Have I checked the facts, figures and references?
- Am I still learning and using my own judgement, or just accepting what it says?
- Who do I ask if I'm unsure?

## Your reading route

1. **Know your rules (10 minutes).** Read your organisation's acceptable use policy. If it does not have one yet, the [acceptable use policy template](../../toolkits/acceptable-use-policy.md) shows what one usually covers.
2. **Understand the tools (20 minutes).** Read [capabilities and limitations](../foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md).
3. **Watch a short explainer.** See the [video catalogue](../../videos/README.md), including scripts on [what AI tools can and cannot do](../../videos/scripts/01-what-ai-tools-can-and-cannot-do.md) and [safe everyday use](../../videos/scripts/03-safe-everyday-use.md).
4. **Learn your tool.** If your organisation has chosen a tool, read its [tool guide](../tools/tool-guides/README.md) for features, data controls and known limitations.
5. **Build your prompt habits.** Use and contribute to a [prompt library](../../toolkits/prompt-library-template.md).
6. **Look up unfamiliar terms** in the [glossary](../foundations/glossary.md).
7. **Want to go further?** Read [what are AI tools?](../foundations/what-are-ai-tools.md) and [evidence on impact](../foundations/evidence-on-impact.md).

## Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Pasting in personal or confidential data | Check your policy; remove identifying details; use only approved tools |
| Trusting confident-sounding answers | Verify facts, figures and references |
| Using a personal account for work | Use your organisation's approved tool |
| Using AI for tasks you cannot check | Use it where you have the expertise to judge the result |
| Accepting the first draft | Iterate, edit and make it yours |
| Letting it do all the thinking | Use it to support your judgement, not replace it |
| Keeping mistakes quiet | Report them so others can learn |

## What good looks like

- You use approved tools, with your work account.
- You know which data you can and cannot enter, and you follow that rule without exception.
- You use AI for tasks that suit it, and check outputs in proportion to the stakes.
- You still apply your own expertise and judgement, and keep building your skills.
- You share prompts and tips that work, and report problems.
- You can explain, if asked, how AI contributed to a piece of work.
