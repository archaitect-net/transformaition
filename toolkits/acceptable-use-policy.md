# Acceptable use policy template: AI tools

> **Who this is for:** Policy owners, IT, data protection, HR and managers who need a staff-facing policy on using AI tools at work. Works for a ten-person business or a large organisation.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

This is a fill-in-the-blanks acceptable use policy (AUP) for staff use of AI tools. It covers scope, approved tools, data classification rules (what may and may not go into which tools), verification duties, disclosure, prohibited uses, incident reporting and training. Guidance notes in blockquotes explain choices you need to make; replace the [bracketed placeholders] and delete the guidance before issuing. It is the staff-facing companion to the organisation-level [AI policy template](ai-policy-template.md). Keep it short and specific: staff need to be able to apply it in the moment.

> **Guidance: before you start**
>
> - An AUP works only if it matches reality. Find out which AI tools staff already use, including personal accounts and AI features inside existing software, before you write it.
> - Align the data rules with your existing information classification scheme. If you do not have one, the four-level scheme below is a reasonable starting point.
> - Check consistency with your information security, data protection, records management, social media, and disciplinary policies.
> - Consult staff representatives where you have them, and data protection and legal advisers.
> - The UK National Cyber Security Centre has advised against including sensitive information in queries to public large language models, noting that queries are visible to the provider and may be stored [1]. Business tiers of many tools offer stronger contractual protections; your data rules should distinguish between tool types accordingly.
> - This template is not legal advice.

## 1. Purpose

This policy explains how you may use artificial intelligence (AI) tools in your work for [Organisation name], so that you can benefit from them while protecting our customers, colleagues, information and reputation.

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to:

- all employees, contractors, temporary staff, volunteers and [others] who work for or on behalf of [Organisation name];
- any AI tool used for work, whether provided by us or not, on any device; and
- AI features built into software we already use (for example, writing assistants in email or document software, meeting transcription and summaries, AI features in design or coding tools).

"AI tool" means any software that generates, summarises, transforms, classifies or analyses content using AI, including chat assistants, image, audio and video generators, coding assistants and AI search.

> **Guidance:** If you want a narrower scope (for example, generative AI only), say so explicitly. Many organisations find a broad scope easier to apply because AI features now appear in many ordinary tools.

## 3. Approved tools

You may use only the AI tools listed as approved for work purposes. The current list is maintained at [location, e.g. intranet page].

| Tool | Approved for | Highest data classification allowed | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Tool A, business/enterprise tier, accessed via work account] | [General drafting, summarising, analysis] | [Confidential] | [Sign in with work account only] |
| [Tool B, embedded in existing software] | [Meeting summaries] | [Internal] | [Inform attendees; not for HR or disciplinary meetings] |
| [Coding assistant] | [Software development] | [Internal] | [Code review rules in section 6 apply] |
| Public or consumer AI tools (free or personal accounts) | [Only tasks using Public information] | Public | [Never sign in with work credentials unless approved] |

To request a new tool, contact [team, email or form]. Do not use unapproved tools for work, even if they seem harmless, until they have been reviewed.

> **Guidance:** Decide whether to allow consumer tools at all. Options: (a) prohibit for any work use; (b) allow for Public information only (as above); (c) allow with specific approval. Each has trade-offs: prohibition can push use out of sight, while permitting it requires staff to judge classification correctly. State the position clearly.

## 4. Data classification rules

Before you put any information into an AI tool, check its classification and the tool's approval level.

| Classification | Examples | Public or consumer AI tools | Approved business AI tools |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Published website content, press releases, public reports | Allowed | Allowed |
| **Internal** | Internal procedures, non-sensitive meeting notes, general project information | **Not allowed** | Allowed |
| **Confidential** | Customer or client information, personal data, contracts, financial information, unpublished plans | **Not allowed** | Allowed only in tools approved for Confidential, and only the minimum needed |
| **Restricted** | Special category personal data (e.g. health, ethnicity), criminal records data, passwords and credentials, security details, legally privileged material, [regulated data] | **Not allowed** | **Not allowed** unless a specific use case has been approved through [process] |

**Always:**

- use the minimum information needed for the task;
- remove or replace names and identifying details where the task can be done without them;
- check whether a contract, client agreement or licence restricts how information may be used.

**Never** enter passwords, access keys or other credentials into any AI tool.

> **Guidance:** Replace the examples with your own classification labels. If personal data is involved, your data protection lead should confirm which tools and uses are covered by your records of processing and any DPIA. See [data protection](../docs/governance/data-protection.md).

## 5. Your responsibilities: verification

AI tools can produce content that is wrong, incomplete, out of date, biased, or invented, while sounding confident. You are responsible for any work you produce with AI assistance, just as for any other work.

You must:

1. **Check facts, figures, names, dates, quotations and references** against reliable sources before using them. AI tools can fabricate references that look real.
2. **Check that the output does what you need**: answers the right question, follows our policies and tone, and is appropriate for the audience.
3. **Apply extra care** where an error could affect a person's rights, health, finances, employment or access to services, or where content will be published or sent externally.
4. **Review code** generated by AI before use, including for security and licensing, following [code review standard].
5. **Use your professional judgement.** If you cannot verify an output, do not rely on it.

> **Guidance:** You may want to define levels of review by risk, for example: personal notes (self-check), internal documents (self-check plus normal review), external communications (manager or peer review), decisions about individuals (only with an approved use case and documented human decision).

## 6. Disclosure

- **Externally:** Tell people when they are interacting directly with an AI system (for example, a chatbot) rather than a person, and label AI-generated images, audio or video presented as real, as required by [law / our standards].
- **For substantive content:** Disclose AI assistance where [our clients / regulators / publishers / academic or professional bodies] require it, and in these cases: [list, e.g. reports to clients, published research, tender responses].
- **Internally:** Be open with your manager and colleagues about how you used AI in your work when asked, and when it is material to how the work should be reviewed.
- **Meetings:** Tell attendees before using AI transcription or summarisation, and stop if anyone objects [or: follow the meeting recording policy].

> **Guidance:** Disclosure rules vary by sector and jurisdiction. In the EU, the AI Act's transparency obligations under Article 50 (for example, informing people that they are interacting with an AI system, and labelling certain synthetic content) apply from 2 August 2026 [2]. Many organisations do not require disclosure of routine assistance such as grammar suggestions. Decide what "substantive" means for you and give examples.

## 7. Prohibited uses

You must not use AI tools to:

1. enter information in breach of section 4;
2. make or substantially determine decisions about individuals (such as recruitment, performance, discipline, credit, eligibility for services) unless the use case has been specifically approved through [process];
3. create content that is unlawful, discriminatory, harassing, defamatory or misleading;
4. create deepfakes or impersonate any real person, or create synthetic images, audio or video of colleagues, customers or others without their consent;
5. monitor colleagues or customers without an approved purpose and legal basis;
6. bypass security controls or our acceptable use of IT policy;
7. generate content that infringes copyright or other intellectual property, for example by asking for reproductions of protected works;
8. produce professional advice (for example legal, medical or financial) for others without the review required by [professional standards];
9. use AI agents or automations that send messages, change records, make payments or take other actions on our behalf without approval through [process];
10. [add sector-specific prohibitions, e.g. safeguarding, clinical decisions].

## 8. Accounts, settings and security

- Use your work account for approved tools. Do not use personal accounts for work tasks involving anything other than Public information.
- Do not change admin or data settings on approved tools.
- Be cautious with content from outside the organisation (web pages, inbound emails, uploaded documents) processed by AI tools: it can contain hidden instructions intended to manipulate the tool. Report anything unusual.
- Do not install AI browser extensions, plug-ins or add-ins without approval.

## 9. Reporting problems

Report immediately to [contact / form / service desk] if:

- you think Confidential or Restricted information has been entered into an unapproved tool;
- an AI tool produces harmful, discriminatory or seriously wrong output that has been or could be relied on;
- an AI tool behaves unexpectedly (for example, revealing information you should not see).

Reporting promptly helps us limit harm. We will treat honest reports of mistakes as an opportunity to learn. [Align with your incident and disciplinary policies.]

## 10. Training

You must complete [AI essentials training / module name] before using approved AI tools for work, and refresher training [annually / when this policy changes]. Training records are kept by [team]. See [training plan](training-plan-template.md).

## 11. Breaches

Breaches of this policy may lead to withdrawal of access to AI tools and may be dealt with under [disciplinary policy / contract terms]. Serious breaches involving personal data may need to be reported to the [Information Commissioner's Office / relevant supervisory authority].

## 12. Questions and help

Contact [name, team, channel]. Practical guidance, examples and approved prompts are available at [intranet / prompt library location].

## Quick reference card (optional, one page for staff)

> **Guidance:** Many organisations find a one-page summary is read far more than the full policy. Adapt and distribute this.

**Before you use AI at work, ask:**

1. **Is the tool approved?** Check [list location].
2. **Is the data allowed?** Public: any approved tool. Internal or Confidential: approved business tools only. Restricted: not without specific approval. Never credentials.
3. **Will I check the output?** Verify facts, references and figures. You are responsible for the result.
4. **Does anyone need to know?** Disclose where required; tell people if they are talking to AI.
5. **Is this a prohibited use?** No decisions about people, no deepfakes, no impersonation, no unapproved automations.
6. **Something wrong?** Report it to [contact]. Early reporting helps.
