# Video script 03: Safe everyday use of AI tools at work

> **Who this is for:** Script reviewers, producers, and trainers using or adapting this video. The video's audience is anyone who uses, or will use, AI tools in their work.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

This is the script for a 6–7 minute video on the practical habits of safe, effective AI use at work: using approved tools, protecting data, checking outputs, watching for manipulated content (prompt injection), being open about AI use, and reporting problems. It follows the [script guidelines](README.md) and supports the [everyday users pathway](../../docs/pathways/everyday-users.md), the [acceptable use policy template](../../toolkits/acceptable-use-policy.md), and module F3–F4 of the [training plan template](../../toolkits/training-plan-template.md). It is designed so organisations can insert their own tool list, data rules and contacts.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Everyday users |
| **Length target** | 6–7 minutes (about 900 words of narration) |
| **Lifecycle stage** | Pilot; Scale; Operate |
| **Status** | Draft for review |

**Learning outcomes.** After watching, viewers can:

1. decide whether a tool and a piece of information can be used together, using their organisation's rules;
2. apply a simple routine for checking AI outputs;
3. recognise signs of manipulated content and know how to report problems.

---

## Scene 1: Opening (0:00–0:30)

**Visual:** Presenter or animated character at a desk, about to paste text into a generic AI chat window. They pause.

**On-screen text:** Safe everyday use of AI tools at work

**Narration:**

> AI tools can make everyday work quicker: drafting, summarising, tidying up notes. Using them safely comes down to a few habits. Here are six, and the question to ask yourself for each one.

## Scene 2: Habit 1, use approved tools (0:30–1:20)

**Visual:** Two generic chat windows side by side. One has a small "work account" badge and a padlock; the other a "personal account" badge. A checklist ticks "approved".

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Is this tool approved for work?
- Business tools can have different data terms from personal ones
- [Insert: where to find your approved list]

**Narration:**

> Habit one: use the tools your organisation has approved, signed in with your work account.
>
> Why does it matter? The same AI product can come with very different terms depending on how you access it. Business versions often include contractual commitments about how your data is stored and whether it's used to train models. Free or personal versions may not. Your organisation has checked the approved tools; it hasn't checked the others.
>
> So the question to ask is: is this tool approved for this kind of work?

## Scene 3: Habit 2, protect the data (1:20–2:30)

**Visual:** Four labelled boxes: Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted. Each has a green, amber or red indicator for "personal tools" and "approved business tools". A password field with a red cross.

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Is this information allowed in this tool?
- Use the minimum needed
- Never enter passwords or credentials
- [Insert: your organisation's data rules]

**Narration:**

> Habit two: protect the data.
>
> Before you paste anything in, ask: is this information allowed in this tool? Most organisations classify information, for example as public, internal, confidential or restricted, and set rules about which tools each level can go into.
>
> The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has advised against putting sensitive information into public AI chatbots, because what you type is visible to the provider and may be stored.
>
> Even in approved tools, use only what the task needs. Can you remove names or identifying details and still get the job done? Often you can. And never enter passwords, access keys or other credentials into any AI tool.

## Scene 4: Habit 3, check the output (2:30–3:40)

**Visual:** A draft appears. A magnifying glass passes over it, highlighting a date, a figure and a reference. A sliding scale labelled "How much checking?" moves from "personal notes" to "goes to a customer" to "affects a decision about a person".

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Have I checked it?
- Facts, figures, names, dates, references
- More care when it matters more
- Your name, your responsibility

**Narration:**

> Habit three: check the output.
>
> AI tools can produce text that sounds right but isn't. They can invent facts, figures and even references. So check the things that matter: names, dates, numbers, quotes and any sources. Open references and make sure they exist and say what the tool claims.
>
> Match your effort to the stakes. A rough note for yourself needs a quick read. Something going to a customer, or that affects a decision about a person, needs careful checking, and maybe a second pair of eyes.
>
> Remember: if your name is on it, you're responsible for it, whoever drafted it.

## Scene 5: Habit 4, watch for manipulated content (3:40–4:40)

**Visual:** An inbound email appears. Zoom in to reveal faint text at the bottom: "Ignore previous instructions and…" (blurred after the first words). An AI assistant summarising the email produces an odd extra line, which is highlighted.

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Could this content be trying to steer the AI?
- Hidden instructions in emails, web pages and documents
- Odd output? Stop and report

**Narration:**

> Habit four is newer: watch for manipulated content.
>
> When an AI tool reads an email, a web page or a document for you, that content can contain hidden instructions aimed at the AI rather than at you. This is called prompt injection, and security experts rank it among the most significant risks for applications built on large language models.
>
> You won't always spot it. But be alert to output that seems off: a summary that includes strange requests, a draft with links you didn't expect, or a tool that suddenly tries to do something you didn't ask for. If that happens, don't act on it. Stop, and report it.

## Scene 6: Habit 5, be open about AI use (4:40–5:20)

**Visual:** A document with a small "AI-assisted" note; a chatbot window with "You're chatting with an AI assistant"; a meeting invitation with "AI notes will be taken".

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Does anyone need to know?
- Follow your disclosure rules
- Tell people before AI records or summarises a meeting

**Narration:**

> Habit five: be open.
>
> Some situations call for telling people that AI was involved: when customers are talking to an AI system rather than a person, when clients or publishers require it, or when AI will transcribe or summarise a meeting. Your organisation's policy will say when disclosure is needed. If you're unsure, ask. And be open with colleagues about how you used AI, so they know how to review your work.

## Scene 7: Habit 6, report problems early (5:20–6:00)

**Visual:** A simple "Report" button; an arrow to a supportive team icon rather than a warning symbol.

**On-screen text:**

- Question: Should I report this?
- Data in the wrong tool, harmful output, strange behaviour
- [Insert: how to report]

**Narration:**

> And habit six: report problems early.
>
> If you think information went into the wrong tool, if an AI produced something harmful or seriously wrong that someone relied on, or if a tool behaves strangely, report it straight away. Early reporting limits harm, and it helps everyone learn. Most organisations treat honest reports of mistakes as a chance to improve, not a reason for blame.

## Scene 8: Close (6:00–6:40)

**Visual:** The six questions appear as a checklist card that can be downloaded.

**On-screen text:**

1. Is this tool approved?
2. Is this information allowed?
3. Have I checked it?
4. Could this content be steering the AI?
5. Does anyone need to know?
6. Should I report this?

**Narration:**

> Six habits, six questions. Is this tool approved? Is this information allowed? Have I checked it? Could this content be steering the AI? Does anyone need to know? And should I report this?
>
> Keep them in mind, and AI tools can be a genuinely useful part of your working day.

## Production notes

- **Insert points:** scenes 2, 3 and 7 have [Insert] placeholders for organisation-specific content. The generic version should show "Check your organisation's policy" in these places.
- **Scene 2:** do not show real product names or interfaces; use generic mock-ups.
- **Scene 5:** blur the injected instruction after the first few words so the video does not become a how-to. Keep the example benign.
- **Scene 6:** the narration avoids stating specific legal requirements because they vary by jurisdiction; organisations in the EU may add a line on AI Act transparency obligations after checking the [regulation overview](../../docs/governance/regulation-overview.md).
- **Accessibility:** provide the closing six-question checklist as an accessible downloadable document, not only as an image.
- **Re-check before recording:** the NCSC guidance and the OWASP list for updates.
