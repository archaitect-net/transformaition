# Video script 01: What AI tools can and cannot do

> **Who this is for:** Script reviewers, producers, and trainers using or adapting this video. The video's audience is everyone in an organisation, with no technical background assumed.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

This is the script for a 6–7 minute explainer on how generative AI tools work in plain terms, what they tend to be good at, where they fail, and what that means for how people use them at work. It is scene-by-scene, with visuals, on-screen text, narration and sources, following the [script guidelines](README.md). It supports the [capabilities and limitations](../../docs/foundations/capabilities-and-limitations.md) page and module F1–F2 of the [training plan template](../../toolkits/training-plan-template.md).

| | |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Everyone |
| **Length target** | 6–7 minutes (about 900 words of narration) |
| **Lifecycle stage** | Discover |
| **Status** | Draft for review |

**Learning outcomes.** After watching, viewers can:

1. describe in plain terms how a generative AI tool produces text;
2. give examples of tasks these tools often help with, and tasks where they are unreliable;
3. explain why outputs must be checked, and what "checking" means in practice.

---

## Scene 1: Opening (0:00–0:30)

**Visual:** Presenter, or animated title over a simple office desk scene. A chat window appears with a question and an answer typing out.

**On-screen text:** What AI tools can and cannot do

**Narration:**

> AI tools that write, summarise and answer questions are now part of everyday software. Used well, they can save time and help people think. Used badly, they can produce confident nonsense. In the next few minutes we'll look at how these tools work, what they're good at, where they go wrong, and what that means for you.

## Scene 2: How they produce text (0:30–1:40)

**Visual:** Animation. A sentence builds word by word; above each next position, a small bar chart shows several candidate words with different probabilities. One is selected; the sentence continues.

**On-screen text:**

- Trained on very large amounts of text
- Predicts a likely next piece of text, again and again
- Plausible is not the same as true

**Narration:**

> Most of the AI writing tools you'll meet are built on what are called large language models. A large language model has been trained on a very large amount of text. From that training it learns patterns: which words and ideas tend to follow which.
>
> When you type a request, the model produces its answer one small piece at a time. At each step it works out which next piece of text is likely, given everything so far, picks one, and repeats. That's how it can write fluent paragraphs on almost any topic.
>
> Notice what this means. The model is producing text that is *plausible*. It is not looking facts up in a database unless the tool has been specifically connected to one, and even then it can misread or misquote what it finds. Fluent and confident is not the same as correct.

## Scene 3: What they tend to be good at (1:40–2:40)

**Visual:** Four simple icons appear in turn with short examples: a page with a pen (drafting), a long document shrinking (summarising), arrows between two shapes (transforming), a lightbulb (brainstorming).

**On-screen text:**

- First drafts
- Summaries of material you provide
- Rewriting, restructuring, translating
- Ideas and options to react to

**Narration:**

> So where do these tools tend to help? Generally with language tasks where a good-enough first version is useful and a person checks the result.
>
> Drafting: a first version of an email, a report section or a job description that you then improve.
>
> Summarising: condensing a long document or a meeting transcript you've provided.
>
> Transforming: rewriting something in plainer language, changing its tone, turning notes into a table, or translating.
>
> And brainstorming: generating options or questions you might not have thought of, for you to judge.
>
> Research supports some of this, with important conditions. In one large randomised study of management consultants, those using an AI tool completed tasks within its capabilities faster and to a higher standard on average.

## Scene 4: Where they go wrong (2:40–4:00)

**Visual:** Same four icons, now each with a warning marker. Then a mock-up of a citation list in which one reference is highlighted as fabricated (clearly labelled "Illustration").

**On-screen text:**

- Invented facts and references
- Out of date or missing information
- Bias from training data
- Errors on tasks just outside their abilities

**Narration:**

> Now the limitations.
>
> First, they can make things up. Because they generate plausible text, they sometimes produce facts, quotations or references that don't exist. This is often called "hallucination". In a well-known US case in 2023, lawyers were fined after filing a court document containing case citations a chatbot had invented.
>
> Second, their knowledge can be out of date or missing, especially about your organisation, unless the tool has been connected to current information.
>
> Third, they can reflect biases in the data they were trained on, in what they say and in what they leave out.
>
> And fourth, and this one's subtle: they can be unreliable on tasks that *look* similar to ones they do well. In that same consultants study, on a task designed to fall just outside the tool's capabilities, people using AI were less likely to reach the right answer than people working without it. The researchers described AI's abilities as a "jagged frontier": strong in some places, weak in others, and not always where you'd expect.

## Scene 5: Responsibility doesn't transfer (4:00–4:50)

**Visual:** A 2024 news-style card (generic, no logos): "Tribunal: company responsible for what its chatbot told a customer." Then a simple graphic: AI output → person checks → decision.

**On-screen text:**

- The organisation is responsible for what its AI says
- You are responsible for work you produce with AI

**Narration:**

> If an AI tool gets something wrong, who's responsible? In a 2024 case in Canada, an airline argued it wasn't liable for incorrect advice its website chatbot gave a customer. The tribunal disagreed: the company was responsible for the information on its website, whether it came from a static page or a chatbot.
>
> The same principle applies at work. If you use AI to help produce something, it's still your work. The tool doesn't take the responsibility; you do.

## Scene 6: What checking means (4:50–5:50)

**Visual:** A checklist animates on, item by item.

**On-screen text:**

- Check facts, figures, names and dates
- Open and read every reference
- Ask: does this answer the actual question?
- Take more care when errors would matter more

**Narration:**

> So what does checking actually look like?
>
> Check facts, figures, names and dates against a reliable source. If the tool gives you a reference, open it and make sure it exists and says what the tool claims. Ask whether the output actually answers the question you had, for the audience you have. And scale your effort to the stakes: a rough internal note needs a quick read; something going to a customer, or affecting a decision about a person, needs much more.
>
> One more trap: when a tool is usually right, it's tempting to stop checking. That's exactly when errors slip through.

## Scene 7: Close (5:50–6:30)

**Visual:** Return to presenter or title scene. Three summary points appear.

**On-screen text:**

- Fluent ≠ correct
- Great for drafts, summaries, ideas
- You check, you decide, you're responsible
- Next: Safe everyday use of AI tools at work

**Narration:**

> To sum up. AI tools generate plausible text, which makes them fluent but not reliably correct. They're often genuinely useful for drafting, summarising, rewriting and generating ideas. But their strengths and weaknesses are uneven, so you check the output, you make the decisions, and you remain responsible.
>
> To learn the practical rules for using these tools safely, watch the next video in this series.

## Production notes

- **Scene 2 animation:** keep it conceptual. Do not imply the model "looks up" words in a dictionary or database.
- **Scene 4 illustration:** the fabricated citation must be visibly labelled "Illustration" and must not resemble a real case name.
- **Scene 5:** use a generic news-card style with no airline branding.
- **Accessibility:** narration already describes the key visual points; check that on-screen text stays visible for at least two reads.
- **Re-check before recording:** whether newer research materially changes the scene 3–4 summary; see [evidence on impact](../../docs/foundations/evidence-on-impact.md).
- **Adaptation point:** in scene 6, organisations can add their own verification rules or link to their [acceptable use policy](../../toolkits/acceptable-use-policy.md).
