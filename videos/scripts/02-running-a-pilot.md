# Video script 02: Running a pilot that gives you an answer

> **Who this is for:** Script reviewers, producers, and trainers using or adapting this video. The video's audience is managers, team leads and pilot leads planning a trial of an AI tool.
> **Last reviewed:** 2026-10

This is the script for a 6–7 minute video on how to run an AI pilot that ends in a clear, evidence-based decision rather than drifting into unmanaged use. It covers choosing the use case, setting hypotheses and baselines, choosing participants, controls, measuring (and why self-reports are not enough), stop criteria and the final decision. It follows the [script guidelines](README.md) and supports the [Pilot stage](../../docs/lifecycle/04-pilot.md) and the [pilot plan template](../../toolkits/pilot-plan-template.md).

| | |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Managers, team leads, pilot leads |
| **Length target** | 6–7 minutes (about 900 words of narration) |
| **Lifecycle stage** | Pilot |
| **Status** | Draft for review |

**Learning outcomes.** After watching, viewers can:

1. write testable success measures for a pilot before it starts;
2. explain why a baseline and objective measures are needed, not just user opinion;
3. set stop criteria and describe the three possible end decisions.

## Scene 1: The problem with most pilots (0:00–0:45)

**Visual:** Animated timeline. A pilot starts with fanfare; weeks pass; the end date quietly disappears and the bar extends indefinitely. A question mark hovers over "So, did it work?"

**On-screen text:** Pilot started → … → "So, did it work?"

**Narration:**

> Many AI pilots end the same way. People liked the tool, or some did. A few found it really useful. Nobody is quite sure whether it saved time or improved anything. And the pilot never formally ends: people just keep using it.
>
> A pilot is an experiment. Its job is to give you an answer: should we scale this, change it, or stop? This video is about how to design a pilot that actually gives you that answer.

## Scene 2: Start with a specific use case (0:45–1:30)

**Visual:** A wide, vague label "Try AI" shrinks and is replaced by a specific card: "Draft first replies to routine customer enquiries: Customer Service team (fictional example)".

**On-screen text:**

- Not "try AI"
- A specific task, team and outcome
- Data and risks checked first

**Narration:**

> Start with a specific use case, not "let's try AI". A good pilot use case names the task, the team and the outcome you care about. For example: drafting first replies to routine customer enquiries in one team.
>
> Before you start, check the basics. Is the data the tool needs available and appropriate to use? Has someone looked at the risks? Is the tool approved, and are its settings configured? The pilot plan template in this guide has a readiness checklist for exactly this.

## Scene 3: Decide what success looks like, in advance (1:30–2:30)

**Visual:** A table builds: Hypothesis | Measure | Threshold. Rows: "Faster drafting | minutes per reply | at least 20% less (fictional)"; "Quality maintained | audit score | no decrease"; "No serious incidents | incident log | zero".

**On-screen text:** Write it down before you start

**Narration:**

> Next, decide what success looks like, and write it down before the pilot begins.
>
> Turn your hopes into hypotheses you can test. For instance: using the tool reduces the time to draft a reply by at least a fifth. Quality is maintained or improved. And there are no serious incidents.
>
> Always include at least one measure of quality or risk, not just speed. Faster isn't better if the replies are wrong.
>
> Why write this down first? Because if you decide what counts as success after you've seen the results, it's very easy, without meaning to, to pick the measures that look good.

## Scene 4: Measure the baseline, and don't rely only on opinions (2:30–3:40)

**Visual:** Split screen. Left: a stopwatch and a sample audit sheet labelled "Before". Right: the same labelled "During pilot". Then a speech bubble saying "It definitely made me faster!" next to a chart where the measured bar is longer, with the label "Illustration of one study's finding".

**On-screen text:**

- Baseline first
- Combine measured data with surveys
- Self-reports can be wrong

**Narration:**

> To know whether something improved, you need to know where you started. Measure the baseline before the pilot: how long the task takes today, how good the output is, how many errors get through.
>
> Then measure the same things during the pilot. Use system data where you can, short time logs where you can't, and a quality check on a sample of real outputs.
>
> Surveys are useful for understanding how people feel. But don't rely on them alone. In one small randomised study published in 2025, experienced software developers using AI tools took longer to complete their tasks, yet believed the tools had sped them up. It was a small study in a specific setting, so don't over-generalise it. But it's a good reminder that how fast something *feels* isn't the same as how fast it *is*.

## Scene 5: Choose participants and controls (3:40–4:40)

**Visual:** A group of figures: some enthusiastic, some neutral, some sceptical, varying experience. Then a simple diagram of a human reviewer between the AI draft and the customer.

**On-screen text:**

- Mix of experience and attitudes
- Comparison group if you can
- Human review and quality sampling

**Narration:**

> Who takes part matters. If your pilot group is made up only of enthusiasts, results won't tell you much about everyone else. Include a mix of experience levels, and some healthy sceptics.
>
> If you have enough people, a comparison group doing the same work without the tool helps you separate the tool's effect from other changes, like a quiet month. In smaller organisations, a before-and-after comparison with the same people is fine; just be cautious about what you conclude.
>
> And put controls in place from day one: who reviews outputs before they're used, how you'll sample quality, and how people report problems. Give participants training and a place to share tips and issues.

## Scene 6: Stop criteria and the end decision (4:40–5:50)

**Visual:** A traffic-light graphic with three outcomes: green "Scale", amber "Change or extend", red "Stop". A calendar with a fixed decision date circled.

**On-screen text:**

- Set a decision date
- Pause if: serious incident
- Decide: scale, change, or stop

**Narration:**

> Two more things to agree before you start.
>
> First, stop and pause criteria. If something serious happens, for example confidential information is exposed, or a harmful error reaches a customer, who can pause the pilot? And at the halfway point, what result would make you stop early?
>
> Second, a fixed decision date and three possible outcomes. Scale, if your success measures were met and the risks are acceptable. Change or extend, if results are mixed and you have a specific reason to expect improvement, such as better data or a revised process. Or stop, if it didn't work.
>
> Stopping is a valid result. A pilot that tells you clearly not to invest has saved you money. Whatever you decide, record it, share what you learned, and if you stop, remove access.

## Scene 7: Close (5:50–6:30)

**Visual:** Summary checklist animates on.

**On-screen text:**

- Specific use case
- Success measures written first
- Baseline + measured data
- Mixed participants, real controls
- Decision date: scale, change or stop
- Template: Pilot plan (toolkits)

**Narration:**

> So: pick a specific use case. Write down what success looks like before you begin. Measure a baseline, and use real data as well as opinions. Include a mix of people and put controls in place. And set a date to decide: scale, change, or stop.
>
> You'll find a pilot plan template with all of this in the toolkits section of this guide.

## Production notes

- **Scene 2–3 example:** label the customer-service example and thresholds as fictional on screen.
- **Scene 4:** the chart is an illustration of the direction of the finding, not a reproduction of the study's figures; label it accordingly. Narration must keep the caveat about sample size and setting.
- **Re-check before recording:** METR has indicated its 2025 results are out of date and published a later update; check whether the later findings change how scene 4 should be framed, and update the source if so. See [evidence on impact](../../docs/foundations/evidence-on-impact.md).
- **Accessibility:** narration states the key point of each chart; on-screen tables should be simplified to three rows maximum for legibility on phones.
- **Adaptation point:** organisations can add their own approval route and the name of the person who can pause a pilot in scene 6.
